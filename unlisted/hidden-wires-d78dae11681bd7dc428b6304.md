This post describes an analogy[^1]: using a Large Language Model (an AI system like ChatGPT or Claude, for example) is in some ways like going to the theatre: you see what happens on stage, but what you see is just a small slice of reality. In this post we talk about some of the things that are not "visible" when you use such a system.
{: .notice}


## 1. The AI Performance: "What's onstage?"

<b>What do you see? What story does it tell you?</b>

A <i>child</i> goes to the theatre and sees Peter Pan fly.<br>

An <i>experienced spectator</i> goes to the same show, and sees the same thing. They receive the same story, and at the same time they know there are wires that make this possible. If they are not fully absorbed by the show, or if the production is not ideal, they might look for those wires and see them..<br>

A <i>theatre professional</i> goes to the same show, and seeing Peter Pan fly reminds them  how the harnesses work, how the safety checks work, and how the stage, lights, and movement have all been constructed and choreographed to make those wires minimally visible.<br>
All three can still enjoy the performance.

What performance does an <b>LLM (Large Language Model)</b>  give its audience? Current LLM's don't fly with wires, but they can help with tasks such as writing and editing, explanations and tutoring, translation, planning and organization, how-to instructions, brainstorming, computer coding, decision-making support, explaining bureaucratic and legal forms, and numerous other activities. Though the performance was carefully constructed, the benefits are still real[^2].

Still, the audience sees only the performance, not everything required to produce it. Becoming AI literate means learning about what is not visible. So... what happened before the curtain rose, and what is happening backstage?



## 2. The AI Production: "How was it produced?"

<b>What happened before the curtain rose? How was this performance created? Who else was involved?</b>

The theatre piece probably involved a writer writing the script (possibly after extensive research and reading), casting actors, sourcing props, building sets, testing effects and visibility from the audience point of view, rehearsing scenes, receiving notes from a director, and likely many years of other work leading up to that point. 

The LLM's reply to your question or prompt rests on enormous quantities of earlier human and digital work: collection and selection of training data; inclusion and exclusion of particular languages, cultures, sources, and viewpoints; training and testing; implementation of safety guidelines; development of the system's "voice"; and a myriad of other elements. Even the "empty stage" is far from empty: in some sense, the stage is the "interface" that you use: a screen with possibility for microphone input, keyboard input, a search feature, organizing your threads by 'projects', a box in which you enter your prompt, and so on. All of this shapes your experience, while not necessarily being the focal point of your conscious attention.

## 3. The AI Business: "Who benefits from producing it?" 

<b>Who owns this production? Who paid for it? What are their goals? What counts as a successful performance?</b>

Productions cost money. They may be funded by ticket sales, government arts councils, wealthy patrons, universities, or corporate sponsors. This does not _necessarily_ mean the play is bad, or that its message is dictated by its funder. But funding does affect which productions are possible, target audiences, and "success" metrics. The audience sees the play; it usually does not see the grant application, contract, budget meeting, or negotiations that occurred prior to performance.

AI systems can cost billions of dollars. Companies may earn money through subscriptions, API access, partnerships, advertising, anticipated profits, to name a few sources. They work with investors (who might want growth), governments (who might fund, purchase, regulate, or use), universities (employing academic researchers who may contribute ideas, evaluations, employees, and being public legitimacy while pursuing grants, publications, and prestige). A comprehensive account of this network is far outside the scope of this short post.

These interests are neither necessarily sinister nor necessarily "aligned" with social and ethical values. A system can help you while also helping its company. Free services do not automatically mean that your personal data is being sold. But they do mean that it is worth asking what exchange makes the free service worthwhile to its provider.

But all this is to say that "your private conversation with your AI system" is taking place within a much larger technical and organizational framework. Providers may collect prompts, feedback, device information, and usage patterns. This information may in turn be used for aspects including security, evaluation, personalization, and business decisions. Important backstage information—such as training data sources, internal instructions, commercial agreements, safety decisions, and evaluation methods—are typically hidden away: proprietary, secret, difficult to explain, or simply unavailable to the audience.

This does not mean that someone backstage is secretly controlling every line. It simply means the performance has incentives and constraints not visible in the performance itself. This is true for just about all media that we consume. Thus, as with media literacy, AI literacy involves asking not only, "Is this useful to me?", but also: Who produced it? Who paid for it? What is being measured? And what would success look like from _their_ seat?


## 4. The AI Audience: "What happens to us as we watch?"

<b>How does the performance affect its audience? What might we begin to expect from the world outside the theatre?</b>

A performance does not simply show us something; it can gradually change our sense of "normal". Actors were carefully cast for particular roles based on perceptions and expectations, wear makeup and costumes, stand under carefully designed lighting, and are viewed from a distance. Their appearance may come to feel ordinary even though it is the result of selection and construction. 

Similarly, relationships on stage or on screen may be emotionally recognizable, while being a symbolic version of a real relationship. Months of uncomfortable uncertainty get compressed into a couple of short scenes. Dialogue is sharp and an uninteresting stretch of time disappears. The performance can teach us about life and relationships, while remaining divorced from real life (it is not a substitute).

LLMs, too, can create expectations. They can respond quickly, remain focused on us, have limitless patience. They can break the fourth wall and address you by name and remember details about you, appearing concerned, curious, and highly interested in what you have to say. You might momentarily feel "seen", which can be compelling and might in turn reinforce you to continue your "relationship" with it, and lead you to increase your usage habits. Some habits can be genuinely helpful (e.g. for learning or  organizing or other forms of support), but they also risk replacing reflection, human contact, or seeking appropriate professional advice. This is perhaps more complicated than ordinary "screen addiction"; the screen now adapts to us---it actively participates in building the relationship that brings us back for more.

## 5. So what do we do?

We do not need to stop enjoying the show. The child, the experienced spectator, and the theatre professional can all enjoy when Peter Pan flies. Similarly, an AI-literate audience can benefit from the performance while maintaining critical questions. Why does this feel so persuasive? (Does the polished writing make it sounds more "expert" than it is?) What habits and expectations is it creating in me? Do I remember that I am watching a show, and wm I choosing to continue—am I in a willing or an unwilling suspension of disbelief? Or something else altogether?
